# Motion-design research for creative planning

**Reviewed:** 2026-11-13. This is a short, cited synthesis to improve the
LLM-led workflow, not an aesthetic scoring model. External examples are for
abstract study only: do not copy their frames, timing, scripts, assets or brand
identity. Read these alongside `REFERENCE_LIBRARY.md`,
`PRO_TYPOGRAPHY_SYSTEM.md`, `MOTION_DESIGN_SYSTEM.md` and `REVIEW_RUBRIC.md`.

## What transfers into this product

1. **Design the still before polishing motion.** Typography, framing, hierarchy,
   negative space, balance and contrast are graphic-design decisions first.
   Motion cannot rescue an unconvincing frame. Ask the host model for distinct
   styleframes / key moments before it specifies detailed animation.
2. **Every beat needs a job.** Build a concise visual argument: hook or question,
   relevant context/tension, demonstration or evidence, consequence/payoff, then
   a clean resolution when the brief warrants one. Do not force a CTA or formula
   onto stories that do not need it. An opening is a promise that the film must
   actually pay off; never invent proof to make a hook stronger.
3. **Stage one dominant action at a time.** Direct gaze using composition and
   temporal hierarchy. Secondary movement must clarify, connect, or add a
   deliberate emotional layer; peripheral decoration competes for attention.
4. **Block timing before adding polish.** Establish the intended change, entrance,
   complete readable hold, and departure. Vary duration with content and visual
   weight; avoid uniform shot lengths and wall-to-wall motion. A pause can be the
   strongest beat. No source supports a universal timing number for every film.
5. **Use a coherent grammar with purposeful variation.** Reuse brand typography,
   color, and motion logic so the piece belongs together; vary framing, density,
   tempo, and transitions only when the story changes. Same layout plus different
   preset is not a new art direction.
6. **Critique the delivered evidence.** Review storyboard stills for graphic
   quality, motion strips / playback for timing and continuity, and the encoded
   MP4 for final typography, audio, flashing and compression. These are different
   checks. A contact sheet does not prove motion or audio quality.
7. **Treat mobile and accessibility as hard constraints.** Judge type at target
   size; protect the stable hold, safe zones and contrast. Inspect the encoded
   output for flashing. W3C's three-flashes criterion is a specific safety
   standard, not a general aesthetic test; interaction-triggered WCAG motion
   criteria should not be mislabeled as a universal requirement for linear MP4.

## Applying the evidence carefully

The sources do not agree on one style or exact timing. Several discuss interface
motion, not short-form film; their lessons on attention, staging and sequencing
are transferable, but their UI durations are **not** imported as video defaults.
Short-form marketing articles proposing hook lengths are treated as hypotheses,
not evidence that a fixed hook formula improves this system's outcomes.

The product loop should therefore use two passes:

- **Cheap creative blocking:** concept alternatives, chosen visual thesis,
  representative styleframes, scene/beat map and animatic timing.
- **Evidence-based finishing:** actual Studio render, version-bound critique,
  minimal revision, hard QA and explicit human acceptance on target device with
  sound.

Keep all recommendations defeasible: a still, slow or text-free opening can be
right when the brief and proof support it. Reject an output for a specific,
visible failure, not for violating a template.

## Sources consulted

- [Jess Riley, “The Principles of Motion Design,” Envato Tuts+, 2024-06-14](https://design.tutsplus.com/the-principles-of-motion-design--ytc-79c) — graphic hierarchy, balance/tension, contrast, negative space, rhythm; block storyboard timing before keyframes; easing, anticipation and follow-through. Practitioner guidance, not an experimental result.
- [Liz Blazer, *Animated Storytelling*, chapter “Storyboarding Hints,” Pearson / Peachpit, 2019-08-13](https://www.peachpit.com/articles/article.aspx?p=2979068&seqNum=2) — shot size, framing, staging and visual hierarchy. Source was read; no reference frames or timecodes imported.
- [Liz Blazer, *Animated Storytelling*, chapter “Is It Really Working? Timing and Animatics,” Pearson / Peachpit](https://www.peachpit.com/articles/article.aspx?p=2979068&seqNum=4) — test the storyboard as an animatic against the intended runtime and sound before finishing.
- [Figma Learn, “Motion design fundamentals: Sequencing”](https://help.figma.com/hc/en-us/articles/41239278373271-Motion-design-fundamentals-Sequencing) — delays should have a reason; offsets and overlap create choreography. Interface/prototype guidance only; no fixed values copied into film rules.
- [Nielsen Norman Group, Page Laubheimer, “The Role of Animation and Motion in UX,” 2020-01-12](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-purpose-ux/) — motion attracts attention and irrelevant motion distracts. UX context, used only for the attention/staging implication.
- [Adobe, “12 Principles of Animation”](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/animation/discover/principles-of-animation.html) — staging, anticipation, timing, follow-through and appeal; these are craft vocabulary, not a recipe to apply all effects everywhere.
- [W3C WAI, WCAG 2.2 Understanding SC 2.3.2 Three Flashes](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/three-flashes.html) — current safety criterion and scope.
- [W3C WAI, WCAG 2.2 Understanding SC 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html) — scope is motion triggered by interaction; avoid misapplying this to all prerecorded video.
